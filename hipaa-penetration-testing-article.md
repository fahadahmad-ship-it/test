# The Quiet Work of Protecting Patient Data: Why Hospitals Now Hire Hackers

Every time a patient fills out an intake form, refills a prescription, or logs in to a health portal, a trail of deeply personal information moves across computer systems. That information, known in regulatory language as electronic protected health information, or ePHI, has become one of the most valuable prizes for criminals online. Stolen medical records often sell for more than stolen payment card numbers, in part because a diagnosis or a history cannot simply be canceled and reissued the way a bank card can.

The numbers explain why hospital leaders increasingly treat data security as a clinical priority rather than an afterthought. According to IBM's 2024 Cost of a Data Breach report, the average healthcare breach cost organizations 9.77 million dollars, keeping healthcare the most expensive industry for data breaches for the fourteenth year in a row. Financial services, the next closest sector, averaged 6.08 million dollars.

Against that backdrop, a growing number of hospitals, medical device makers, and health technology startups are turning to a practice that sounds counterintuitive at first: they pay skilled professionals to break into their own systems.

## What HIPAA actually requires

The federal law that governs this space is the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, passed in 1996 and known to most people simply as HIPAA. Its Security Rule requires any organization that handles ePHI to protect the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of that data. One of the rule's required provisions, listed at 45 CFR 164.308(a)(1)(ii)(A), is a thorough risk analysis.

Notably, HIPAA never uses the phrase penetration test. The law describes outcomes, not tools. It asks organizations to evaluate their safeguards and understand where patient data could be exposed, then leaves the method of doing so to each organization. That space between what the law requires and how an organization satisfies it is where hands on security testing enters the picture.

## Breaking in, on purpose

A penetration test is a controlled, authorized simulation of a real attack. Security specialists probe networks, web applications, and connected medical devices the way a criminal would, documenting every weakness they find before anyone with harmful intent does. Firms that offer [hipaa penetration testing](https://bluegoatcyber.com/services/hipaa-penetration-testing) scope their work to the systems that create, store, or transmit patient data, then map each finding back to the Security Rule so the results feed directly into the risk analysis the law demands.

The approach borrows from established industry frameworks. Web application assessments, for example, commonly measure a system against the OWASP Top 10, a widely referenced list of the most critical web application security risks maintained by the Open Worldwide Application Security Project. Testers also examine business logic and look specifically for places where protected health information might leak into the wrong hands.

## Regulators are watching the method, not just the paperwork

Regulators have made clear that the paperwork of compliance is not enough on its own. The Department of Health and Human Services, through its Office for Civil Rights, enforces HIPAA and has sharpened its focus on risk analysis in particular. In a recent enforcement initiative centered on ransomware cases, the office identified inadequate risk analysis as the common failure across multiple settlements, which together totaled more than 1.1 million dollars in payments.

Office for Civil Rights guidance, first issued in 2010, stresses that no single method guarantees compliance and points organizations toward resources from the National Institute of Standards and Technology. It also declines to set a fixed testing schedule, advising instead that organizations repeat their analysis whenever their systems or circumstances change.

## Why it matters to patients

For patients, this activity is invisible, and that is largely the point. A security program that works well produces no headlines at all. But the stakes are real. A breach can expose mental health histories, HIV status, genetic information, and other details that cannot be undone once they are public.

For the organizations themselves, experts emphasize that testing is not a one time event. Systems change, new devices connect to networks, and attackers refine their methods. A single test captures only a moment in time, which is why specialists recommend repeating the exercise regularly and after any major change to an environment.

The broader lesson, security professionals say, is cultural. Protecting patient data is no longer solely the job of an IT department tucked away in a back office. It now touches clinicians, administrators, and the third party companies that handle data on a hospital's behalf. In an era when a single weak password can compromise thousands of records, hiring someone trustworthy to find that weakness first has quietly become one of the more important forms of patient care.
