# The Other Quincy: What the 3 a.m. Shift Knows That the Rest of Us Don't

The river is black and quiet at three in the morning. Broadway is empty. Most of Quincy is asleep.

Deena Harkness is not.

She is forty minutes into a twelve hour shift, coffee number two going cold beside her, doing the math every overnight worker eventually learns to do. Four more hours until the sky turns gray. Six until she can drive home against the morning traffic she will never be part of. She has worked nights for nine years. She still has not gotten used to it, and she will tell you flat out that nobody really does.

"People think you flip a switch," she says. "You don't. Your body always knows it's supposed to be asleep. You just argue with it and hope you win."

There is a whole second Quincy that runs while the first one dreams. Nurses. Line workers. Truckers pulling off the highway for a few hours of dead sleep in a cab. Dispatchers, bakers, the people who keep a hospital breathing and a plant floor moving. They are easy to miss precisely because their whole job is to be invisible, to have everything working by the time the rest of us wake up.

## The science is not on their side

Our bodies run on a clock older than any factory whistle. Mess with it and the bill comes due.

Researchers at the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health have spent decades documenting what night workers feel in their bones. Their [work on shift work and long hours](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/work-hour-training-for-nurses/longhours/mod2/06.html) links chronic night schedules to worse sleep, slower reaction times, and a long list of health risks the day crowd never has to think about. The fix they recommend is almost cruel in its simplicity. Sleep more. Keep a schedule. Protect your dark hours.

Easier said than done when your dark hours are high noon and the neighbor is mowing his lawn.

## Everybody's got a system

Ask around and you learn that overnight workers are amateur scientists of their own exhaustion. Everybody's got a system. Everybody swears by it.

Blackout curtains, taped at the edges so not a sliver of July sun gets through. White noise machines. A hard rule about no screens on the drive home, because the light tricks the brain into thinking the day is starting. One trucker Harkness knows keeps the same wind down playlist he has used since 2011. Another worker naps in two shifts, split around the kids' school pickup, and claims it is the only thing that saved her marriage.

The supplement shelf gets a lot of traffic from this crowd too, and they are a tougher audience than most. They have tried the gentle stuff. Melatonin. Chamomile. Magnesium powders stirred into water that tastes like chalk.

Harkness went further down that road than most. A coworker had mentioned trying [lab-tested kratom from Authentic Kratom](https://www.authentickratom.com/), and what caught her attention was not any promise on the bottle. It was the testing. She is a nurse. She reads things. So she did what her mother taught her to do with anything that goes in your body. She looked it up, asked her own doctor the plain questions, and started slow and careful. That, she says, is the only way she will try anything.

"I'm not telling anybody what to take," she says quickly. "I'm telling them to ask questions first. That's the whole job of being a grown up."

## The part nobody sees

Here is what gets lost in a conversation about sleep hygiene and shift differentials. These people are not statistics. They are your neighbors.

The woman who checked your father's vitals at four in the morning so he was stable by sunrise. The guy who ran the line that made the part that is sitting in your truck right now. The baker whose bread was already warm when the first commuter walked in. We benefit from the overnight shift constantly and think about it almost never, which is exactly how the overnight shift prefers it, and also, maybe, a little unfair.

Muddy River readers know this community runs on people who show up, whether the story is about a [local worker going above and beyond](https://www.muddyrivernews.com/category/news/) or a [business keeping its doors open through hard stretches](https://www.muddyrivernews.com/category/business/). The night crew is the quietest chapter of that same story.

## Daylight

By the time Harkness clocks out, the river has turned silver and the first cars are stacking up on the bridge. She drives home into a world waking up. She pulls the taped curtains. She lies down while the rest of Quincy pours its first cup of coffee.

She does not call herself a hero. She would roll her eyes if you did. She calls it a job, one with lousy hours and good insurance and the strange pride of knowing the town held together one more night partly because she was awake for it.

Sleep well, Quincy. Somebody made sure you could.
