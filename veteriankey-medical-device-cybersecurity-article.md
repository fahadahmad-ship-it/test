# The Next Category of Equipment Error: Cybersecurity and Patient Safety in the Connected Veterinary Practice

Veterinary anesthesia has long accepted an uncomfortable truth: the machines meant to keep patients safe can also harm them. A frequently cited retrospective analysis of roughly 83,000 anesthetics found reported equipment or technical problems in about 0.23 percent of general anesthetics, and nearly a third of those events caused some degree of harm. The recurring culprit was rarely the hardware alone. In a large share of cases, the real failure was human: a skipped pre-anesthetic check, unfamiliarity with the device, a monitor trusted without being understood.

That body of evidence built the modern culture of equipment vigilance, the checklists, the pre-use verification, the insistence that a clinician know the instrument in front of them. A new failure mode is now quietly joining the list, and it does not announce itself on any pre-anesthetic checklist. It is the vulnerability that arrives when a clinical device is also a networked computer.

## Why Connectivity Is a Patient Safety Issue, Not Just an IT Issue

It is tempting to file cybersecurity under office technology, alongside the practice management software and the Wi-Fi. For the devices that touch patients, that filing is a mistake.

Contemporary [patient monitoring and clinical measurement](https://veteriankey.com/patient-monitoring-and-clinical-measurement/) increasingly depends on software, wireless links, and integration with records systems. A multiparameter monitor is no longer a self-contained box displaying a waveform. It is a small computer running firmware, often connected, often capable of being updated, and therefore capable of being disrupted. When a device that governs an anesthetized patient behaves unexpectedly, the distinction between a mechanical fault and a software one is academic. The patient is at risk either way.

This is precisely why the established literature on [equipment and technical errors in veterinary anesthesia](https://veteriankey.com/4-equipment-and-technical-errors-in-veterinary-anesthesia/) is the right frame for thinking about cybersecurity. The profession already understands that device failure endangers patients and that most such failures trace back to how people interact with the equipment. Software compromise is not a foreign concept dropped onto the clinic floor. It is the newest branch of a problem veterinarians have studied for decades.

## The Familiarity Gap, in a New Form

One of the more striking findings in veterinary patient safety is how often harm stems not from broken equipment but from inadequate familiarity with it. In one analysis of contributing factors, insufficient familiarity with equipment outweighed outright equipment failure by a wide margin. Clinicians are hurt less by machines that break than by machines they do not fully understand.

Connectivity widens that familiarity gap. Few veterinary teams know which of their devices are networked, which still run outdated firmware, which were shipped with default passwords that were never changed, or how long a manufacturer will continue to issue security updates. These are not obscure technical trivia. They are the modern equivalent of not knowing how a vaporizer behaves, and they sit in the same blind spot that patient safety work has spent years trying to illuminate.

The reassuring part is that the remedy is cultural before it is technical. The discipline that produced the pre-anesthetic checklist is exactly the discipline that secures a connected clinic.

## What the Standards From Human Medicine Make Clear

Veterinary equipment is not yet governed by dedicated cybersecurity regulation, but much of it is shared with or derived from human medical technology, where the rules have advanced rapidly. That makes the human-side framework the most credible reference available to any veterinarian trying to understand what good looks like.

Regulators and standards bodies have formalized a lifecycle approach to securing medical devices, one covering design, market entry, and the full span of use that follows. Current expectations require manufacturers to show how a device will resist compromise before it is sold and how vulnerabilities will be managed afterward. These [medical device cybersecurity standards](https://bluegoatcyber.com/blog/guide-to-medical-device-cybersecurity-standards-premarket-postmarket-lifecycle) trace a device across its entire life, from premarket submission through postmarket monitoring and patching. The questions they force a manufacturer to answer, how the device is protected, how it is updated, and how long it will be supported, are the same questions a veterinary buyer should put to a vendor, even where no regulation yet compels it.

## Folding Cybersecurity Into Existing Safety Culture

None of this requires a veterinary team to become a security operation. It requires extending habits the profession already holds. The literature on [patient safety](https://veteriankey.com/introduction-to-patient-safety/) stresses verification, redundancy, and knowing your equipment, and each principle maps directly onto the digital domain.

Keep devices and their software current, because unpatched systems are the most common point of entry. Change default passwords and limit access to those who need it, the digital version of controlling who operates an unfamiliar machine. Maintain tested backups, the single most effective defense against the ransomware that can freeze a practice mid-procedure. Segment the network so one compromised device cannot reach the rest. And weigh a vendor's security posture, including its update commitment, alongside clinical specifications at the moment of purchase. Each of these is a checklist item in the same spirit as checking the anesthetic machine before induction.

## A Quiet Extension of an Old Responsibility

Veterinary medicine has always treated the reliability of its equipment as a clinical duty rather than a technical afterthought. The security of connected devices belongs to that same tradition. As practices lean harder on networked monitors, imaging systems, and integrated records, the integrity of that technology becomes inseparable from the safety of the patients who depend on it.

The frameworks built for human medical devices offer a clear map of what responsible security looks like across a device's life. Veterinarians do not need to wait for their own regulations to learn from them. The next anesthetic disaster averted by a checklist may be a digital one, and the clinics that recognize cybersecurity as a patient safety issue, rather than an IT inconvenience, will be the ones ready for it.
