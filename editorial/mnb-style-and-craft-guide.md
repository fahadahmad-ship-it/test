# Medical News Bulletin — Tone Profile & Craft Guide

**Purpose:** everything needed to write a piece that reads like it belongs on
medicalnewsbulletin.com (MNB), and that survives the two audiences every health
story now has — a skimming human and a retrieval system deciding what to quote.

**Evidence base:** MNB's own About page, plus ~40 live headlines, decks and lede
fragments across its categories, gathered October 2026. Direct page fetching was
blocked by this environment's network policy, so sourcing is the public search
index rather than article-by-article reading. Treat the structural claims
(headline grammar, categories, sourcing habits) as solid and the fine details
(exact word counts, bylines, review process) as unconfirmed — check a live page
before treating any of them as house rule.

---

## 1. What MNB actually is

Published by Wisdom Medical Inc. Its stated mission is "improving public
understanding of biomedical and health sciences," and the editorial promise that
matters most is this one:

> "Our writers strive to go beyond the press release to show you the evidence."

That single line is the whole brief. MNB is not a wire service reprinting
university PR, and it is not a wellness blog. It sits in the narrow, valuable
middle: **single-study explanatory journalism, written for a curious non-expert,
that says what the study actually showed and what it did not.**

Observed categories:

| Section | What runs there |
|---|---|
| Latest Health and Medical News | The core: one new study, explained |
| Medicine / clinical specialties | Cancer, cardiology, neurology, diabetes |
| Discover → **Misinformation Busters** | "What's the deal with seed oils?", "…with ivermectin and cancer?" |
| Nutrition briefs | Short-form: avocado and cognition, energy drinks and 5k times |
| Fitness / lifestyle | Pilates and running, activity trackers |
| Explainers | "What Is Gestational Diabetes?", "How Epidemiologists Turn Research Into Public Health Action" |
| Opinion / clinician voice | "Doctors, Stop Telling Your Patients, 'It's Normal'" |
| Funny Medical | Light relief; rare |

Two tells worth copying. First, MNB runs **roundups with teeth** — its 2026
breast cancer roundup covers three advances *and* flags one study "whose methods
make its results look too good to be true." Second, it runs a standing
**misinformation desk**, which means the house is comfortable naming a bad claim
and taking it apart. Few health sites at this size do either.

## 2. Voice

**The register:** a well-read friend who happens to have read the paper. Plain,
warm, unhurried, never breathless, never patronising. Second person is allowed
and used ("Could Melatonin Help Your Chronic Pain?"). Contractions are fine.

**Non-negotiables of the voice:**

- **Hedge where the evidence hedges, and only there.** MNB's melatonin piece
  lands on "may help" because the review landed on "may help." Equally: when a
  finding is null, say so flatly — the metformin-in-pregnancy piece says
  researchers "did not find any measurable effect," no softening, no "more
  research is needed" mush.
- **Name the journal and the institution in the body.** *Nature Human
  Behaviour*, *Pain*, *JAMA Network Open*, University of Sydney, UCSD. This is
  the "beyond the press release" promise made visible, and it is also what makes
  a passage quotable by an AI system.
- **No miracle, no breakthrough, no game-changer.** No "shocking." If a result
  is genuinely surprising, let the number be surprising.
- **Never prescribe.** MNB explains; it does not tell a reader to start or stop
  a drug. Point to a clinician.

**Headline grammar — four patterns, all live on the site:**

1. **Direct question:** "Can targeted immunotherapy stop the spread of cancer after surgery?" · "Do Activity Trackers Encourage Physical Activity?"
2. **Finding + attribution:** "Pilates improves running performance, study finds"
3. **"What's the deal with X?"** — reserved for the misinformation desk
4. **Flat explainer:** "What Is Gestational Diabetes?"

Rule for pattern 1: **ask the question only if the piece answers it.** A question
headline over an unanswered body is the single fastest way to lose a reader's
trust, and it is the one thing MNB's own ethos forbids.

## 3. The structure that works

A 700–1,100 word MNB news piece, in order:

**1. Lede (1–2 sentences).** The finding, in human terms. Not "A new study
published in *Pain* examined…" — that is a sentence about a study, not about the
world. Write the thing the reader can repeat to someone else.

**2. Nut graf (2–4 sentences).** Why this, why now, why you. The Open Notebook's
formulation: the nut graf exists to tell the reader why they are being told
about this particular thing today and why they should care enough to keep going.
This is the paragraph most drafts skip and most pieces die without.

**3. What they did.** Who was studied, how many, for how long, compared against
what. Numbers in plain language — "32 trained runners, split into a Pilates group
and a control group," not "an n=32 randomised design."

**4. What they found.** The result, with **absolute numbers**. See §4.

**5. The caveats — in the body, not a footer.** Small sample, short follow-up,
observational so no causal claim, industry funding, surrogate endpoint. MNB's
house move is to put the limitation in the same breath as the finding rather
than quarantining it at the bottom where nobody reads it.

**6. What it means for the reader.** Concrete, bounded, non-prescriptive.

**7. Sourcing.** Journal, authors, DOI or link. Inline links preferred over
footnotes.

**Briefs** (nutrition, fitness) compress this to 250–400 words but drop nothing
except depth — the caveat still ships.

## 4. The numbers rules — this is where health writing actually fails

A German analysis of medical news found **45% of headlines overstated the
conclusions of the paper beneath them.** A study of 734 front-page medical
stories found only 57% were based on mature peer-reviewed research, and fewer
than one in five stories about preliminary findings said so. Almost all of that
damage comes from four avoidable errors.

**1. Relative risk without absolute risk.** "Cuts risk by 50%" can mean 2 in 100
became 1 in 100. The Science Media Centre's rule: give the absolute figure
whenever the paper or release contains it. Goldacre's stronger version: express
it as extra events per 1,000 people. If the release only gives relative numbers,
**calculate the absolute from the paper** — that is the job.

> Write: "Deaths fell from 4 in 1,000 to 3 in 1,000 — a 25% relative drop."
> Not: "Deaths fell 25%."

**2. Correlation stated as cause.** Observational studies produce "linked to,"
"associated with," "more common among." Only a randomised trial earns "reduced,"
"caused," "prevented." Check the design before choosing the verb — this is one
decision, made once, and it determines the honesty of the whole piece.

**3. Mice, dishes and surrogates.** If it was mice, the headline says mice. If
the endpoint was a biomarker rather than an outcome anyone feels, say that the
study measured the marker, not the disease.

**4. Preprint, conference abstract, or peer-reviewed?** State it. One clause.

**Always, in every piece:** sample size · study design · who funded it · how long
the follow-up ran · absolute numbers · what the study could not show.

## 5. Writing for the AI-mediated reader

Health search in 2026 is increasingly answered above the link — AI Overviews and
chat assistants summarise, cite a handful of sources, and send a fraction of the
traffic a blue link used to. Vendor numbers on all of this disagree wildly and
should be treated as directional, not settled. But the editorial implications are
stable and, usefully, they point the same way good journalism already pointed:

- **Summaries of press releases are the most replaceable content on the
  internet.** A model does that for free. What it cannot do is read the
  supplementary tables, call the third author, or notice that the control arm
  changed mid-trial. Originality is now a distribution strategy, not just a
  virtue.
- **Write self-contained passages.** The extractable unit is a paragraph that
  answers one question without needing the three paragraphs above it. Each
  section should survive being lifted out alone.
- **Answer first, then explain.** Inverted pyramid at paragraph level, not just
  article level.
- **Be definitive where you are entitled to be.** Hedged mush gets skipped by
  humans and by retrieval alike. Hedge on the science; never hedge on the
  writing. "The trial found no benefit" beats "results appeared to suggest a
  possible lack of benefit."
- **Date and attribute every claim.** "In a September 2026 *JAMA Network Open*
  paper…" is both better journalism and more machine-legible than "researchers
  recently found."
- **Named author with real credentials, visible publication date, links to the
  primary source.** These are the checkable trust markers, and for health — a
  YMYL topic where the bar is highest — they are not optional.
- **Structure for scanning:** descriptive subheads phrased as the questions
  readers actually ask, short paragraphs, a table or list where there is a
  comparison to make.

The uncomfortable summary: **the floor has risen.** Content that merely restates
a press release has no audience left, because the machine got there first and did
it faster. The pieces that still earn attention are the ones that add a
judgement a summariser cannot make — which is exactly what MNB's "beyond the
press release" line already promised.

## 6. Angles that reliably outperform

- **The caveat nobody else ran.** Everyone covers the finding; one outlet reads
  the limitations section. That piece is the one that gets cited.
- **The null result.** "No effect" is underpublished and highly useful. The
  metformin piece is MNB doing this well.
- **The claim takedown.** Misinformation Busters is MNB's sharpest franchise:
  take a circulating claim, trace it to its source, show exactly where it breaks.
- **The number nobody converted.** Translate a relative risk into "one extra case
  per 2,000 people per year." Readers remember that; they do not remember a
  hazard ratio.
- **The honest "we don't know yet."** Phase 1, n=18, 6-week follow-up — say
  precisely what would have to happen for this to matter.
- **The clinician's irritation** — the register of "Doctors, Stop Telling Your
  Patients, 'It's Normal'." A specific, grounded argument from inside the system.

## 7. Pre-publication checklist

**The journalism**
- [ ] Have I read the paper, not just the release?
- [ ] Design named; verb matches design (associated vs. reduced)?
- [ ] Absolute risk given, not only relative?
- [ ] Sample size, follow-up length and funding in the body?
- [ ] Mice / dish / preprint flagged in the headline if applicable?
- [ ] Limitations next to the finding, not buried at the end?
- [ ] One independent voice who did not write the paper?
- [ ] Does the headline promise exactly what the body delivers?

**The craft**
- [ ] Lede about the world, not about a study being published?
- [ ] Nut graf present, by paragraph three?
- [ ] Jargon either cut or defined on first use?
- [ ] Zero instances of breakthrough / miracle / game-changer?
- [ ] No instruction to start, stop or change a treatment?

**The reach**
- [ ] Each section self-contained enough to quote alone?
- [ ] Subheads phrased as reader questions?
- [ ] Journal, institution and date named in the text?
- [ ] Primary source linked?
- [ ] Named author with credentials and a visible publication date?

## 8. Worked example — lede and nut graf

**Weak (press-release voice):**

> A new study published in the journal *Pain* has investigated the potential
> role of melatonin supplementation in patients suffering from chronic
> musculoskeletal pain. Researchers conducted a systematic review and
> meta-analysis of the available literature.

Nothing happens. It is a sentence about the existence of a paper.

**Strong (MNB voice):**

> Melatonin, the sleep supplement sitting in half the bathroom cabinets in the
> country, may do something its label never claimed: take the edge off chronic
> muscle and joint pain.
>
> That is the cautious conclusion of a University of Sydney systematic review in
> the journal *Pain*, which pooled the existing trials of melatonin for chronic
> musculoskeletal conditions. The effect was real but modest, and the trials
> behind it were small — which makes this less a reason to start taking
> melatonin than a reason someone should finally run a proper trial of it. For
> the roughly one in five adults living with persistent pain, even a modest,
> cheap, low-risk option is worth knowing about.

The second version states the finding, names the journal and institution,
delivers the caveat inside the same breath as the claim, tells the reader why it
matters to them, and does not once tell them to take anything.
